The three indicators, in plain language

  • Saty ATR Levels — a map of what a "normal" move looks like. It takes yesterday's close and the average true range (how far this thing typically travels in a day, week, or month) and draws a ladder of levels around it: a trigger at ±23.6% of that range, the Golden Gate zone from 38.2% to 61.8%, the full range at ±100%, and extensions beyond. Instead of guessing whether a move is big or small, you can see it: price crossing the upper trigger has started a meaningful push; price at +100% has already traveled a full normal day. The levels double as a target ladder and as spots where moves tend to pause.
  • Saty Pivot Ribbon — trend at a glance. It's layered clouds of moving averages (fast ones and slow ones). When the fast layers stack above the slow ones, the trend is up and the ribbon acts like a floor on pullbacks; stacked the other way, trend is down and it acts like a ceiling. When the layers squeeze together, the market is coiling.
  • Saty Phase Oscillator — a momentum gauge that swings between extremes, telling you where in the cycle a move is: just igniting, stretched, or exhausted. Its most useful state for this study is compression — the zone where momentum has gone quiet and energy is building. The "boxes" in the Bilbo study are drawn from exactly those compression candles.

The full written system — setup definitions, level probabilities, operating rules, everything — is documented here on the site: The Trading Manual.

Part 1 — What backtesting is and why the biases matter more than the results

What a backtest is

A backtest is a simulation: you write down a rule precisely enough that a computer can follow it with no judgment calls, then replay history and record what the rule would have done. The output is a distribution of trades — not a prediction, but an answer to a narrower question: "if the future resembles this sample of the past, what should I expect from this rule?"

That narrowness is the point. Before a backtest, a trading idea is a story ("compression leads to expansion"). After one, it's a measurable claim with a sample size attached ("across 2,345 hourly compression boxes on 20 stocks over 24 months, buying the break paid +12.0% of premium per trade"). Stories can't be wrong; claims can — which is what makes them useful.

Why it's important

Three reasons, in increasing order of importance:

  1. It kills bad ideas cheaply. Most ideas don't survive contact with data. Finding that out in an afternoon of simulation costs nothing; finding it out with real money costs the tuition.
  2. It sizes the good ones. Knowing a rule wins 36% of the time with a median trade of −50% changes how you size and how you emotionally prepare. The same strategy traded at 5% of the account and at 20% of the account are different strategies — one of our simulations showed the 20% version ends in ruin on the same trade stream where the 5% version compounds.
  3. It forces precision. "Buy the breakout" is not testable. "Buy the this-Friday ATM call within 5 minutes of a 5-minute bar poking above the box high between 9:30 and 4:00 ET" is. The act of making a rule testable usually reveals that you had three different strategies in your head under one name.

The catch: a backtest is easiest to cheat by accident

Every backtest is a claim about the past made by someone who already knows the past. That's the original sin, and every bias below is a variation of it. A biased backtest is worse than no backtest, because it converts a hunch into false confidence with a t-statistic stapled to it.

The biases that matter most, roughly in order of how often they burn people:

1. Look-ahead bias — the rule accidentally uses information that wasn't available at decision time. It can be blatant (using the day's close to decide a morning entry) or subtle. We hit a subtle one in this study: option expiries were initially anchored to when the compression started, not when the trade was actually entered — so a late entry could be assigned a contract a real trader wouldn't have picked. One line of code, systematically wrong prices.

2. Survivorship bias — testing on the stocks that exist and are big today means testing on history's winners. Our ticker list (NVDA, TSLA, AMD...) is exactly that: names we chose in 2026 because they're huge and liquid in 2026. Over a 2-year window this is a modest distortion; over 20 years it would be fatal. Our IPO study made this vivid from the other side — measuring "buy every IPO" honestly required digging delisted tickers out of a separate data source, because the easy sources only remember survivors.

3. Overfitting / data mining — test enough variants and something will look great by chance. The defense is not statistical cleverness, it's discipline: decide the grid in advance, count how many cells you searched, and treat an isolated positive cell as noise until it repeats out-of-sample. This study produced textbook examples: a MSFT-only positive cell in round one (gone at scale), a credit-selling overlay that showed +15.5% on 3 tickers and −1.3% on 20, and a "retest entry" lead that evaporated when the sample grew 10×. Each looked publishable at the time.

4. Execution and fill assumptions — the quiet killer for options backtests. Our option data is trade prints only (no bid/ask quotes), so the simulation buys and sells at prices someone actually traded at — but not necessarily prices we could have gotten. Real fills pay the spread, and trailing-stop exits fire during fast moves when fills are worst. A strategy whose whole edge fits inside the bid/ask spread is a simulation artifact. That's why the study carries an explicit 2–4 point haircut warning, why every exit was re-tested under worst-case fill assumptions, and why a dedicated bias-recovery pass (with next-print fills, liquidity filters, and spread modeling) is running as a follow-up.

5. Selection and framing effects — which trades get counted. Mixing untradeable signals with tradeable ones can hide a real edge or create a fake one. The single most important discovery in this study was a framing fix: separating overnight gap-breaks (which returned ≈0 on 2,064 boxes) from intraday breaks (+8.3% on 972). Pooled together, the edge was invisible.

6. Sample-dependence and clustering — 369 trades on SPY, QQQ, and SPX sound like a sample of 369, but those three move together; there were only 182 distinct entry days. Statistics that ignore clustering overstate confidence. The same trap exists across time: an edge measured only in 2023–24 is a claim about 2023–24. Where possible we re-check across years and across eras (one validation here went back 20 years on the underlying mechanics).

7. Missing baselines — a number means nothing without a control. Buying random weekly ATM options in this period lost −15.5% per trade (t=−3.25) — theta bleed is the gravity of this asset class. Against that baseline, even a 0% strategy is beating the market's pricing; +12% is remarkable. Judging edges against zero instead of against the random-entry baseline is a way to fool yourself in both directions.

The common thread: the goal of a backtest is not to produce a big number, it's to survive attempts to kill the number. Year splits, out-of-sample tickers, worst-case fills, cluster-robust statistics, adversarial code review — a result that survives all of that has earned some trust. A result that hasn't is a screenshot.

Part 2 — This study: how we actually got here

The original question

The study began 2026-07-07 with a specific, five-part question about hourly Saty Phase Oscillator compression on AMZN, NVDA, and MSFT (12 months of data): when hourly PO compression appears, what's the best (1) direction rule — daily 21 EMA slope or hourly PO slope; (2) entry spot — at the 9/21 ribbon or after expansion confirms; (3) expiry — 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month; (4) strike — ATM or ±0.5/1.0 daily-ATRs out; (5) exit — profit-target grids, scale-outs, stops, hold-to-expiry. Both long premium and short premium (selling the opposite side) were in scope.

The data pull was substantial: every compression episode was matched to real option contracts, and minute-level trade prints were pulled for ~3,000 contracts (later ~29,000) into a local SQLite database.

Round one: a clean rejection

The first full pass answered every one of the five questions with "no":

  • Long premium was negative in every grid cell (best exit: −5.1%, not significant; hold-to-expiry −32.5%).
  • Every direction rule pointed the wrong way — trades that followed the daily 21 EMA slope, the hourly PO slope, or the expansion direction lost −9% to −15%, while fading those signals was +2–3% (not significant).
  • The credit side was also negative. Straddles were flat-to-negative.

The conclusion we wrote down: implied volatility prices hourly compression fairly on these names. No edge. This could have been the end.

Two things kept it alive. First, the random-entry control: random long ATM weeklies lost −15.5% (t=−3.25) in this period, which reframed every number — "flat" straddles were actually beating theta by 15 points. Second, a variant borrowed from the earlier stock study: instead of trading the compression, trade the box breakout — the range of the first five compression candles, entered when price breaks out.

The pivot, and the finding that mattered

The Bilbo-box variant on the same data showed the first real signal, and scaling it up (8 mega-caps × 24 months, extended-hours bar definition, ~2,449 boxes) confirmed it — but only after two corrections that are the real story:

  1. A look-ahead bug caught by adversarial review. We ran the backtest code past Codex (a second AI, used as a hostile reviewer). It caught that expiries were anchored to the compression start instead of the entry moment. Fixed before the headline was computed.
  2. The intraday/overnight split. Breaks that happened overnight (price gapping out of the box before the open) returned ≈0 across 2,064 boxes. Intraday breaks returned +8.3% per trade (t=3.78, n=972) with a fixed +100%/−50% bracket. Mixing the two classes had been hiding the edge.

Dead ends, catalogued

Each of these looked promising at some point and died under a validation test. They're reported dead in the study rather than silently dropped:

  • Retest entries — a lead in round two (+3.6%) that faded to ≈0 at scale.
  • Box-maturity filters — flipped sign between session definitions; noise.
  • PO slope/position/direction on top of the box — added nothing (the falling-slope cell was t=1.9 after mining; not actionable).
  • Scale-out exits — taking partial profits at +50/+80% turned +8.3% into ≈−1%. Banking early trims exactly the tail that pays.
  • Short-premium carry timer — +15.5% on 3 tickers became −1.3% (t=−0.3) on 20 tickers × 24 months.
  • Fading strength inside the box — reliably negative (−6.2%, t=−3.87, n=1,421): the study's most dependable "don't".
  • Index options (SPY/QQQ/SPX) — full pipeline run on 967 index boxes: directional negative, straddle positive but statistically unconfirmed once clustered by entry date (t=1.74 on 182 distinct days). Index volatility carries the variance risk premium; the movement this trade harvests is only underpriced on single names.

The exit work, and the out-of-sample test

A take-profit × stop-loss grid showed the stop level (−40/−50%) is load-bearing while the profit target is a wide plateau — which suggested removing the target entirely. The arm-then-trail exit (stop −50%; once premium doubles, trail 30% off its high) raised the result to +14.7% per trade (t=4.84) and passed a year-split, all 8 tickers, and a parameter plateau (arm anywhere +75–150%, trail 25–35%).

Then the decisive test: 12 tickers the strategy had never touched (PLTR, COIN, HOOD, INTC, MU, BAC, and others — 1,493 fresh boxes). The fixed bracket collapsed to −0.7% (t=−0.39). The trail held at +11.2% (t=3.95), positive on 9 of 12 names. That split is the single most instructive result in the study: the entry finds opportunities, but most of the harvestable return lives in the exit mechanics, and only the trail generalized to unseen names.

Understanding why (so we're not just curve-reading)

A 20-year validation on the underlying (10,764 box breaks since 2004) showed the break direction predicts almost nothing in any era — win rates 49–54% — while the excursion after a break is large in every era: median ~1.2 daily-ATRs of movement-in-some-direction within the week. The strategy is buying movement cheaply, not calling direction. That frame then predicted a result we hadn't tested: a straddle (both legs, each with its own stop and trail) should work about as well without a direction call. It does — +12.0% of combined premium (t=8.42, 57% win rate, 2,345 boxes), with no decay between the original and held-out ticker sets. Follow-up cuts: expiry × stop tuning is a flat +11–12.3% plateau (tuning closed); VIX regimes split the versions (directional strongest under VIX 16, statistically indistinguishable from noise above 20; straddle solid at both extremes).

Part 3 — The question that reopened everything

With the study published, a plain definitional question came up in review: what exactly counts as a breakout? If three grey candles have formed and a fourth pokes above their range while still grey, is that a breakout — or does it just widen the box? Chasing that question exposed the study's deepest bias: the backtest decided whether each hourly bar was "box material" or "break-watch territory" using that bar's closing state — information that arrives up to an hour after the moment you'd have to act. Rebuilt as a strict live walk (every decision from completed bars only, with an exact intrabar recomputation of the compression flag validated against 80,000 historical bars), the published fixed-bracket headline collapsed from +9.1% per trade to +1.0%. Half the entries a live trader would have taken were "drift pokes" — price wandering out of a frozen box with the squeeze still on — and they earned nothing.

Nine rebuilt entries later, one rule survived: enter at the close of the first candle that both closes out of compression and broke the box. Every faster alternative (intrabar detection, shorter boxes, lower-timeframe confirmation, bail-and-re-enter) either false-fired or paid for its speed in fake-outs. And the survivor's exits had to change character too: the trail that had been "the edge" under level fills turned out to be partly a machine for donating spread — under real quotes, less management is monotonically better, all the way down to a stop and nothing else.

The stock detour that explained the options

Testing the same signal on the underlying itself — no options at all, exits keyed to the stock's own levels — produced the cleanest result of the project: +38.8 bps per trade at strict execution, positive in six of eight years, and a blind-selection protocol (rules chosen on 2019-22, evaluated on 2023-26) that replicated to the basis point. That reframed the entire options failure: the directional edge was always real, roughly 40-55 bps of stock over three days; weekly at-the-money options simply cost more than that in theta and spread. An honest benchmark cut the celebration short — buy-and-hold of the same hindsight-selected universe beat the unlevered strategy on raw return — but the strategy's Sharpe (≈1.9 vs 1.2) monetizes through leverage, and options are the natural leverage. The question was never whether options, but which.

The moneyness × tenor map

The answer turned out to be a surface, mapped cell by cell: strikes from 2 ATRs in-the-money to 1.5 ATRs out, tenors from ~9 to ~45 days, every cell priced on the same 3,833 trades with real NBBO quotes. The shape: a diagonal ridge. Deep-in-the-money works short-dated (almost no time value to bleed); at-the-money needs ~a month (so a loser still has salvage value at the stock-level stop); further out-of-the-money needs both more time and more tolerance for ugly medians. The only toxic corner is out-of-the-money weeklies — the exact corner the original study lived in. Sizing math picks the winners: growth-optimal (Kelly) analysis showed the highest per-trade means (OTM) support the smallest positions, and the boring deep-in-the-money distribution supports positions seven times larger — "expectancy you can't size is expectancy you can't compound."

Management interacts with position on the surface, gently: longer tenors prefer a longer time cap; profit-taking at the strike kills every cell; premium-based stops of any kind (including cutting losers at the end of day one — tested, 50% of the cut trades were next-day winners) make everything worse. Risk lives in the stock's levels and in position size, nowhere else.

Volume, and the bias that hid inside the data feed

Two participation filters, pre-declared before outcomes were read, survived every check: skip the trade if the breakout bar's volume is below that ticker's norm for the same clock hour (monotone across quintiles, era-stable, keeps ~92% of total P&L on 62% of trades), and prefer breakout bars that out-trade their own box. Then the data upgrade delivered a lesson worth the whole exercise: a higher-quality quote feed recovered ~400 trades whose entry minutes had no two-sided quote in the cheaper feed — same contracts, real markets, just gaps — and they averaged −11.5%. The old headline had been quietly flattered by data availability: the missing minutes were fast-market moments when market makers pulled their bids, which is precisely the fake-out profile. The repair and the filter turned out to be the same thing — those recovered losers are low-participation breaks, and the volume gate removes them. With the gates on, every ridge cell clears full quoted-spread execution for the first time in the study: deep-ITM +5.9% per trade at worst-case fills (61% win, +9.3% median), monthly slightly-OTM +8.5%.

Where the system stands

The recipe, in one paragraph: on ~20 liquid high-beta names, wait for an hourly candle that closes out of compression and beyond the box on above-normal volume for that hour, with a real two-sided options market showing; buy either a deep-in-the-money 9-day call (the anchor sleeve — best win rate and median) or a slightly out-of-the-money ~monthly call (the higher-octane sleeve); manage the trade only off the stock's levels — opposite box edge invalidates, half-retrace after 0.75 ATRs of progress takes profit, a few days' cap; size each position at a few percent of the account and let the portfolio, not any trade, carry the outcome.

What remains honest to say: every layer above was validated out-of-sample individually, but the assembled system has not run as a unit anywhere except history; the universe was chosen knowing 2026; and the volume and liquidity gates are now load-bearing, not optional garnish. The live scanner produces these exact signals in real time, and its forward record — where no backtest bias of any kind can operate — is the only remaining test that matters.

Where that leaves us

  • A published study page (milkmantrades.com/bilbo-box-options.html) and a one-page cheat sheet, both carrying the fill-cost caveats.
  • A live scanner producing these signals in real time on the 25 most liquid names, with Telegram alerts — which is the honest next test: paper-trading the signals forward, where none of the backtest biases can operate.
  • An open bias-recovery workstream (Codex-assisted) re-simulating the study under strictly-realistic fills — next-print entries and exits, liquidity filters, spread modeling — to establish which mechanics keep t ≥ 3 under assumptions we could actually trade. Results will land in BIAS_REVIEW.md.
  • Known limits, stated plainly: 24 months is one volatility regime pair (2024-25 chop + 2025-26); returns are % of premium from real prints without spreads or commissions; win rates are low by design and the average is carried by a small number of large runners.

Data sources and tools

Data - Local 5-minute adjusted equity bars, 2003→present, ~3,460 tickers (/srv parquet store; topped up via API). - Massive (Polygon-style) market data API, paid tier: equity/index aggregates (including I:SPX and I:VIX), option contract reference (with expired contracts), and option minute trade prints — the original core dataset: ~29,000 contracts, ~40M minute bars in a local SQLite database. - ThetaData (Theta Terminal v3, upgraded to the Professional tier mid-study): seven years of minute-level NBBO option quotes — real bid/ask with sizes, 2019→present, ~65,000 contracts across the validation reruns and the moneyness × tenor surface. The tier upgrade itself surfaced a bias (see table above): the cheaper feed's missing minutes had been silently excluding the worst trades. - TradingView charts as the visual spec reference for the Saty indicators.

Tools - Python (pandas / numpy / sqlite3) for the pipeline and every analysis; a shared indicators.py implementing the Pine-spec Saty Phase Oscillator, ATR, and EMAs, so studies and the live scanner compute identical signals. - Idempotent, checkpointed fetch pipeline (kill-and-resume safe; it survived an API-tier downgrade mid-pull and a session crash mid-run). - Codex CLI as an adversarial reviewer — code review (caught the expiry bug), design review of the cheat sheet, and now the bias audit. - matplotlib for the PNG cheat sheet; lightweight-charts for the study page's interactive trade examples; cron + Telegram for the live scanner alerts.

The through-line of the whole study: every number that survived did so by outliving an attempt to kill it — a random-entry baseline, a year split, a 12-ticker holdout, a 20-year era check, worst-case fills, a strict live-semantics re-walk, a blind selection protocol, seven years of real bid/ask quotes, a data-feed upgrade that exposed what the cheaper feed had hidden, and two AI reviewers reading the code with hostile intent. The numbers that didn't survive are in the dead-ends list, which is exactly where they belong. And the two most damaging biases were found not by tools but by plain questions — "what exactly counts as a breakout?" and "why does this cell disagree with itself?" — which is the strongest argument this document makes: write your definitions in words a trader can argue with, and chase every number that doesn't reconcile.
