Buy Every IPO?

5 years of US listings — day-1 buying vs waiting it out, by deal size and industry
Universe & rules

Every operating-company IPO on NASDAQ / NYSE / AMEX listed 2021-07-01 → 2026-06-30: common stock and ADRs from the Massive IPO reference feed. 707 SPAC unit offerings and 276 OTC listings are excluded (blank-check shells that slipped through as common stock are segmented out by SIC 6770).

Entry: equal $1 into each IPO at the day-1 opening trade — the first price a retail buyer can actually get (allocation at the issue price isn't realistically available). Delayed variants enter at the close 1, 5, 21, 63, or 126 trading days after listing.

Exit / valuation: held to 2026-05-07 or to delisting (acquisitions exit near deal price, bankruptcies near zero). Delistings stay in the sample — dropping them would fake the results upward.

Benchmark: every position is compared against SPY over the exact same dates ("excess"). Portfolio-level comparisons use money-weighted return (XIRR) on identical cashflow timing.

Verdict

Buying every IPO on day 1 compounded at 3.4%/yr while the same dollars in SPY made 15.6%/yr. Waiting 6 months erases the penalty — but produces no alpha.

The median IPO is down −61% one year after its first trade; only 19% beat SPY over that year. The damage isn't a first-week phenomenon — it accrues steadily through the first year, so waiting 1 week or even 1 month barely helps. By ~6 months (lockup expiry territory) the forward numbers finally match the index.

Both candidate filters are real: deal size is the strongest single filter (sub-$25M listings are near-total capital destruction; $500M+ deals lose only modestly), and industry is statistically predictive (Kruskal–Wallis p≈0.0005) — financial-sector IPOs hold up, services/shipping micro-caps are the graveyard.

Size thresholds don't rescue day-1 buying: even ≥$1B deals compound ~8pp/yr behind SPY bought at the open. Six months of patience brings every size bucket back to market — and the only slice that pulls ahead is non-tech $250M+ deals after the wait (+4pp/yr). Tech IPOs never catch up from any entry.

Key takeaways
  • Never buy the day-1 open indiscriminately. $1 into every IPO compounds at 3.4%/yr vs 15.6% for the same dollars in SPY; the median IPO is −61% a year after listing.
  • Wait at least ~5 months. The entry-delay benefit rises steadily, knees at ~5 months, and is flat out to 12 — "6 months" is convention, not magic. Nothing shorter than ~3 months moves the needle.
  • Size alone doesn't rescue day-1 buying. Even ≥$1B mega-deals lag SPY by ~8pp/yr from the open (and do worse than ≥$500M deals).
  • Skip tech. Tech IPOs lag from every entry; waiting narrows but never closes the gap (big-tech +6-month entry: 4.2%/yr vs SPY 15.2%).
  • The edge cell: non-tech, ≥$250M, bought ~6 months in — 20.6%/yr vs SPY's 16.3% on the same cashflow dates.
  • A −10% stop helps only if the freed cash goes back to work. On the edge-cell entry, stopped-out dollars reinvested equally into the remaining open positions end at $1.69 per $1 (26.8%/yr) vs $1.50 (20.7%) with no stop and $1.38 (16.3%) for SPY — but parked in cash they end at $1.27, worse than no stop. ~3 in 4 positions stop out and returns concentrate in the surviving tail. Promising, not proven.
  • Don't chase pops, don't buy the dip to issue price. "Back at the IPO price" is adverse selection — dip-buyers underperform the simple wait in every cohort, and a stop at issue fires late (~−30%) on 75% of entries.

Method & caveats

Data: Massive (Polygon) IPO reference + daily adjusted bars from the local parquet store, Massive API, and Yahoo (validated to-the-cent against the parquet store on overlapping tickers). SIC industry codes from SEC EDGAR submissions with Massive ticker details as fallback. SPY benchmark from the same stores.

Coverage: 705 of 882 qualifying IPOs (80%) have usable price history. 33 of the missing listed after the valuation cutoff (excluded by design). The remaining ~144 are delisted names, median deal $58M, whose history is temporarily unavailable — they skew small and dead, so published medians are, if anything, optimistic, and the small-bucket numbers especially so. The ≥$100M segment is missing 76 delisted names (~21%) — its true numbers are likely somewhat worse than shown.

Returns are price returns (no dividends) for both the IPO leg and the SPY leg — consistent, but understates SPY total return by ~1.3%/yr of yield. Ticker-reuse splices are guarded by requiring the first bar within 7 days of the official listing date and truncating any >30-calendar-day gap.

Delayed-entry samples shrink with delay (an IPO must survive to the entry point) — that's the real, implementable strategy, not a bias. Horizon returns for delisted names use the last trade as terminal value.

Industry test: Kruskal–Wallis on 1-year excess across the 8 sectors with n≥15: H=26.1, p=0.00049. Much of the sector effect is a deal-size proxy (finance IPOs are big, services micro-caps are tiny); a size-controlled sector test was not significant at n available.
